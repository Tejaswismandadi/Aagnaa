The career narrative
Engineering teaches you how things fail. Supply chain risk management teaches you what to do before they do.
Most supply chain risk professionals come from procurement or operations. They know processes. What they sometimes miss is the physical intuition — understanding why a supplier's quality system is degrading before the data confirms it, reading a facility and knowing which single-source components are going to create problems, understanding how a design change at Tier 3 cascades through a supplier network in ways that a pure data analyst would miss.

Twelve years of engineering — in automotive stamping, closure systems, and aeronautical components — produced that intuition. The engineering training is not background. It is an active input into every risk assessment.
Two engagements that built AGNA
The REI framework and the CBAM regulatory engine — built in practice, not in a textbook.
At Nexteer, through the Ross School of Business Consulting Studio: the Risk Exposure Index. The framework that combines node criticality (TTR/TTS gap) with program revenue exposure into a single prioritised action list. Leadership adopted it. It is the scoring layer that runs through every step of AGNA's Chapter 3 methodology.

At Kearney: the CBAM regulatory compliance tool. 800+ suppliers across 7 European countries. A Power BI tool that maps how a single regulatory trigger propagates through a supplier network — what AGNA calls the Regulatory Trigger Propagation Engine. The insight that cascade exposure is always larger than the sum of individual compliance costs. That framework is Chapter 2's Risk 14 in AGNA.
From practice — not from a textbook
"The hardest part of building a risk management system is not the methodology. It is the first conversation with leadership where you explain why the organisation needs one."
In that conversation, leadership will almost always say one of three things. They will say "we already know our risks" — which means they know their Tier 1 suppliers and have a concern list, but they have never run a TTR/TTS test on a single node. They will say "we cannot afford this right now" — which means nobody has ever put a number on what a P1 disruption costs per day, so the risk of inaction feels abstract. Or they will say "our procurement team handles this" — which means risk management is buried inside a function that does not have the cross-functional authority to act on what it finds.
They say
"We already know our risks."
→
The response
If your most critical single-source supplier went offline today, how many days before your production line stops? When nobody can answer that question, they do not know their risks — they have a list of concerns.
They say
"We cannot afford this right now."
→
The response
Your last disruption cost $180M. The risk management programme costs $4M. That is a 45:1 return on investment. The question is not whether you can afford the programme. The question is whether you can afford another $180M event.
They say
"Our procurement team handles this."
→
The response
When the Taiwan disruption hit, how long did it take to approve the emergency air freight? 11 days. A procurement team without cross-functional authority and pre-approved escalation thresholds cannot act fast enough when it matters.
The way through all three objections is the same question: "If your most critical single-source supplier went offline today, how many days before your production line stops — and how long before you could restore supply from anywhere?" When nobody in the room can answer that question, the case for building the system makes itself. The cost of not knowing is always higher than the cost of knowing.
— Tejaswi Mandadi · from the Nexteer REI engagement and the Kearney CBAM work · Novi, Michigan
