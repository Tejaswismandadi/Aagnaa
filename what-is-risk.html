What is risk

Most companies treat 'things that worry us' as risks. They are not.

Most companies treat 'things that worry us' as risks. They are not — and that distinction is where risk management either begins or stays broken.
A precise definition everyone in the organisation can use
A risk is a future event with an uncertain outcome that could cause measurable harm to the business if it occurs. Three words carry all the weight: future, uncertain, measurable.

If it already happened — it is an incident. Manage it as a lesson learned.
If it is certain to happen — it is a crisis. Execute your contingency plan today.
If you cannot measure the harm — you cannot prioritise it. Quantify before acting.
The probability spectrum — where a concern becomes a risk becomes a crisis
Probability = 0
"Our supplier might be hit by a meteor." No plausible trigger, no precedent.
Not a risk — remove it
The event cannot happen in any realistic scenario. Keeping it on the register wastes attention and budget. Delete it entirely.
Probability = 1 (certainty)
"Our supplier confirmed in writing they close next month."
Not a risk — it is a crisis
Risk requires uncertainty. Once certain, it leaves the risk register and enters the incident response queue. Stop modelling it. Execute your contingency plan today.
Probability between 0 and 1
"Our supplier might have a fire." Possible. Not certain. Not impossible.
Possible risk — test the second condition
Uncertainty exists. Now check: if this happens, does it cause measurable harm? If yes to both — you have a real risk. Score it and manage it.
The two-condition rule — both must be true simultaneously
Condition 1: Probability is between 0 and 1
The event is uncertain — not impossible, not certain. Only the uncertain middle belongs on a risk register.
Condition 2: Impact is greater than zero
If it happens, it causes real measurable harm — production loss, cost, delay, penalty, reputational damage.
NovaDrive result
67 concern items reduced to 41 real risks after applying the two-condition test. 26 items removed — either probability was zero (no realistic trigger) or impact was zero (full redundancy or contractual pass-through). Every mitigation dollar now goes to real risks.
How we measure it

Probability and impact tell you what. Velocity tells you when. You need all three.

A single label tells you very little about a risk. Three numbers scored honestly and combined tell you almost everything.
The Risk Priority Number — three dimensions, not one
Most risk registers assign a single "high / medium / low" label based on gut feel. That is how NovaDrive labelled their Taiwan semiconductor exposure as "medium" — right up until it cost them $180M. A complete risk score requires all three dimensions scored independently.

Risk Priority Number (RPN) — scored 1 to 729
Probability — how likely is the event to occur
Scored 1 to 9. Based on historical data — how often has this type of disruption occurred at comparable suppliers or in comparable regions? Score 1 means it has never happened. Score 9 means it has happened multiple times in recent history.
Impact — how much harm does it cause if it occurs
Scored 1 to 9. Measured in revenue at risk per day of disruption multiplied by estimated duration. A disruption halting $45M of daily production for 180 days scores 9. Must be quantified — not estimated as "high" by feel. This is the number connected to the program revenue master built in Chapter 3.
Velocity — the dimension most companies never score
Velocity is not how fast the event happens. It is how much time you have between the first detectable signal and the moment the risk reaches its full business impact. Two risks with identical P and I scores can demand completely different responses — because one gives you weeks and the other gives you hours.
Sudden onset
Hours to days. By the time you detect it, the damage is already running. Cannot design the response in real time.
Response: pre-built playbook, activates on first signal
Natural disaster Cyber attack Port closure

Weeks to months. Signals exist and are catchable — but only with active monitoring in place.
Response: early warning monitoring, staged intervention

The engineering connection — DFMEA and supply chain risk use identical logic
If you have worked with DFMEA, you already know this framework intuitively. Grounded in SAE J1739.
DFMEA (engineering)
Occurrence (O)How often the failure occurs
Severity (S)How bad the failure effect is
Detection (D)How hard the failure is to catch
Supply chain risk
Probability (P)How likely the disruption is
Impact (I)How much harm it causes
Velocity (V)How fast it reaches full impact
O × S × D = RPN (DFMEA) mirrors exactly P × I × V = RPN (supply chain)
In DFMEA, a failure mode with Occurrence = 0 is removed — it cannot happen. Severity = 0 gets no corrective action — it causes no harm. Supply chain risk follows the exact same discipline. The engineering foundation is not a workaround — it is the academically correct approach.
The uncertainty line

Some risks can be measured. Others cannot. Knowing which is which changes everything.

Some risks can be measured. Others cannot. Knowing which is which changes everything about how you respond — and the tools you use.
Frank Knight's line — drawn in 1921, still defining the field today
In 1921, an economist named Frank Knight drew a line that risk managers still use today. On one side: risks you can measure and assign a probability. On the other side: true uncertainty — events so unprecedented that no probability can be meaningfully assigned. Applying the wrong tools to the wrong side of this line creates false confidence, which is more dangerous than having no system at all.
Knightian risk — you CAN estimate probability
Historical data exists. You can assign a meaningful number. Actuaries, insurers, and risk models are built for this territory.
"This supplier type has experienced a major disruption once every 8 years. Annual probability ≈ 12%."
Tools that work: FMEA · REI scoring · heat maps · Monte Carlo · Altman Z-score
Knightian uncertainty — you CANNOT estimate probability
No reference class exists. The event has never happened at this scale. Standard risk tools are completely blind here.
"What is the probability a novel virus shuts down global automotive supply chains for 18 months?"
Tools that work: resilience capability · scenario planning · survival window analysis · flexibility buffers
Black swans — the most dangerous subset of Knightian uncertainty
Not every uncertain event is a black swan. Nassim Taleb defined the concept precisely: a black swan has exactly three properties, and all three must be present simultaneously. If any one is missing, it is not a black swan.
1
It is an outlier — completely outside all normal expectations
NovaDrive: Full stoppage of leading-edge semiconductor fabrication due to geopolitical escalation had no direct historical precedent at this scale. No risk model had it in its range of scenarios.
2
It carries extreme impact — not marginal, not quickly recoverable
NovaDrive: 4 of 5 programs halted within 6 days. $45M per week in lost production. No qualified alternate source. Estimated time to qualification of an alternate: 14–18 months.
3
After it happens, it feels like it should have been predictable
NovaDrive: Geopolitical tension signals around Taiwan had been reported in trade press for 18 months prior. Three industry analysts had flagged semiconductor concentration risk. The signals existed. No system was watching for them.
Why this distinction matters for AGNA
This is why AGNA separates the risk methodology (Chapters 1–3) from the resilience playbooks (Chapter 4). You cannot put a black swan on a heat map and assign it a probability. The correct response is to build resilience capability — the ability to absorb the shock and recover — before the event occurs. Of NovaDrive's 41 risks: 38 are Knightian risks scored via REI. 3 are black swans managed only through resilience playbooks.
How we classify it

Once you can see a risk clearly, the next question is: where does it live?

Where it lives determines who owns it, how you detect it, and how fast it can reach you.
The four-bucket taxonomy — used by MIT, Sheffi, and ISO 31000
Without taxonomy, a risk register is just a list. With taxonomy, you can assign ownership, detection strategy, and response methodology by category. Visible vs hidden — the secondary classification that matters most
Within each bucket, every risk is classified as visible or hidden.
👁 Visible risks
Detectable through standard supplier monitoring — financial reports, quality scorecards, audits, delivery performance. Standard monitoring is sufficient.
🫥 Hidden risks
Invisible to standard monitoring. Require sub-tier mapping, geopolitical intelligence, regulatory tracking, Altman Z-score analysis, geographic concentration analysis.
NovaDrive's $180M loss
Came from a hidden environmental risk — a Tier 2 semiconductor dependency in Taiwan completely invisible to their standard monitoring system. Their entire monitoring infrastructure was designed for visible risks. This is not unusual. It is the norm. The risks that cause the most damage are almost always the ones nobody was watching for.
Two emerging risks — not yet in standard frameworks

Energy supply as an invisible Tier 0 supplier, and AI dependency as the new single-source risk. Most organisations have not yet incorporated either into their formal risk management systems.
